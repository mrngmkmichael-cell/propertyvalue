What it costs to live there

A listing shows the price. It rarely shows the bills that come with the keys. For any UK address the report puts three of them next to the price, each from the body that publishes it: the council tax bill for every band at that council, the energy costs estimated on the home's EPC, and whether the home is freehold or leasehold. The fourth, the estate charge on a managed development, has no official source at all, and this page says what to do about that.

Bands are fixed fractions of Band D, from six ninths for Band A to eighteen ninths for Band H.

Energy: what the EPC says the home costs to run

Every EPC carries the assessor's estimate of yearly heating, hot water and lighting costs, and what they would fall to after the recommended improvements. The report shows both figures for the address's most recent certificate, as the EPC Register publishes them. They are standardised estimates from the building, not a household's bills, and they are the only like-for-like energy figure that exists for every home in England and Wales.

Tenure: freehold, leasehold, and what comes with it

HM Land Registry records whether each sale was freehold or leasehold, and the report says which. A leasehold home carries ground rent and a service charge set by the freeholder or its agent; those figures are in the lease and the seller's information form, not in any public register, so the report tells you to ask for them rather than guessing.

Estate charges: the running cost no official source publishes

On a development where the council has not adopted the roads, lighting or open space, homeowners pay a private management company for their upkeep on top of full council tax, often for ever, with no cap and, until the 2024 Act takes effect, no right to challenge it. There is no register of what each estate pays.
