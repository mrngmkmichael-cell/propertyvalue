How a UK property purchase works

  1. Get your finances in order. A mortgage Agreement in Principle (AIP) from a lender shows sellers you can actually borrow what you're offering — most estate agents expect one before taking an offer seriously. Budget beyond the purchase price itself: Stamp Duty (or LTT/LBTT), solicitor's fees, survey costs, mortgage arrangement fees, and removals all add up separately.
  2. Find a property and make an offer. Offers in England, Wales and Northern Ireland aren't legally binding until contracts are exchanged (step 5) — either side can walk away up to that point, which is why UK purchases can fall through late. Scotland works differently: an accepted offer there is typically binding much earlier, once missives are concluded.
  3. Instruct a solicitor or licensed conveyancer, and get a survey. Your solicitor handles contracts, searches and the legal transfer; a survey (see Things to check below) tells you about the property's physical condition, separately from your lender's own valuation (which just confirms the property's worth enough to lend against — it isn't a survey and won't flag defects).
  4. Apply for your formal mortgage offer once a price is agreed and the survey's done — the lender needs a specific property and price to underwrite against, not just your general affordability from the AIP stage.
  5. Searches and exchange of contracts. Your solicitor runs local authority, water/drainage and environmental searches (a chunk of what this site's own flood, subsidence, radon, planning and contamination checks let you get ahead of before paying for them formally). Once everyone's satisfied, contracts are exchanged, a deposit is paid, and the purchase becomes legally binding with a fixed completion date.
  6. Completion. The remaining balance is paid, ownership transfers, and you get the keys — typically anywhere from a few days to a few weeks after exchange, by agreement between both sides.
  7. After completion. Your solicitor normally handles submitting the Stamp Duty return and registering the change of ownership with HM Land Registry (or the equivalent in Scotland/Northern Ireland) — worth confirming this is included in their fee, not billed separately.

Tax on buying and owning property

Stamp Duty (or equivalent)

A one-off tax paid on completion, based on the purchase price — Stamp Duty Land Tax (SDLT) in England and Northern Ireland, Land Transaction Tax (LTT) in Wales, Land and Buildings Transaction Tax (LBTT) in Scotland. Rates are banded (you pay each band's rate only on the portion of the price within it, not the whole price at the top rate), and there are different bands for first-time buyers and for additional properties. Bands change with government budgets, so rather than list figures here that can go stale, use the calculator on any property report on this site — it's kept current and does the banding maths for you.

Capital Gains Tax

Applies when you sell a property that isn't your only or main home — a second home or a buy-to-let, typically — on the gain in value, not the sale price itself. Your main residence is normally exempt under Private Residence Relief. Rates and the tax-free allowance change periodically; check gov.uk's Capital Gains Tax on property page for current figures.

Council Tax

An ongoing annual charge from your local council, based on which valuation band the property falls into (A to H in England and Scotland, a different set of bands in Wales) — set by the property's estimated value back in 1991 (England/Scotland) or 2003 (Wales), not its current price. We don't look up a specific property's band here — England and Wales's Valuation Office Agency doesn't publish that as open data, only as a lookup tool — but you can check any address for free at gov.uk/council-tax-bands.

Things to check before you commit

  • Freehold or leasehold. A flat is almost always leasehold; a house usually isn't. A short remaining lease (under about 80 years) gets expensive to extend and can make a property hard to mortgage or resell — always check the years remaining, ground rent, and any service charge before offering.
  • Which survey level you need. A lender's valuation isn't a survey. A Level 1 (Condition Report) suits a newer property in good order; Level 2 (HomeBuyer Report) is the common middle ground; Level 3 (Building Survey) is worth it for older, larger, or altered properties where hidden structural issues are more likely.
  • Everything a report on this site already checks. Flood risk, subsidence (clay shrink-swell), radon, historic land contamination, coal mining risk, planning constraints and heritage designations, sold price history, EPC rating, crime levels, and school catchment — search any address to see it before you're paying survey and search fees to find out formally.
  • Chain position. Buying from a chain-free seller (a new build, probate sale, or someone who's already moved) removes one of the most common reasons a purchase collapses or drags on.
  • Get quotes and get moving early. Solicitor and survey costs vary meaningfully between providers, and both can be booked before you've had an offer accepted — it just shortens the process once you have.
