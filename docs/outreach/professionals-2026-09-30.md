# Ten professionals to write to, and what to write

Researched 30 September 2026. Every address below was read from the firm's
own page or from its entry in the Association of Relocation Professionals'
public member directory on that date, and the page is named so it can be
checked again. Nothing here was bought, scraped from a social network or
guessed from a pattern.

Michael sends these himself, one at a time, from his own mailbox.

## Who is worth writing to, in order

**1. Relocation consultants (best fit).** School search is a service they
sell. They are asked "will we get into that school from this street" every
week, and today they answer it by reading a council PDF. Small firms, one
or two people, who decide in a day.

**2. Buying agents (good fit).** Their value to a client is knowing more
than the client does. A published admission distance, a flood zone and a
sold-price history before a viewing is the kind of thing they repeat to
clients as their own homework.

**3. Mortgage brokers (weaker).** They care about affordability, not flood
maps, but a free report is something they can send a first-time buyer to
look useful at no cost. Treat as a volume play, not a paying customer.

**4. Conveyancing solicitors (weakest, and hardest to reach).** They
already buy official searches from search providers, and this does not
replace one. Their interest, if any, is in fewer surprises before
exchange. Also: of the firms checked on 30 Sep (Winston, Emsleys,
Samuels, Levi), none publishes an email address on its contact page.
They use web forms and phone numbers. That makes cold email the wrong
channel for this category, so it is left out of the first ten.

## The rules that matter

- **The law.** UK cold email to a company, LLP or public body is allowed
  under PECR without prior consent, as long as the sender is identified
  and every message offers a way to opt out. A sole trader or an ordinary
  partnership counts as an individual and needs consent, so if the firm
  is one person trading under their own name, ask first or use their
  enquiry form. Every draft below ends with an opt-out line. Keep it.
- **Send them one at a time**, from the normal mailbox, no mail-merge
  tool, no tracking pixel, no attachment. An attachment from a stranger
  goes to spam; a link does not.
- **Personalise the first line** with something from their own site. One
  true sentence is enough, and it is the difference between a reply and
  a delete.
- **Ten a day at most**, and stop if two in a row bounce.
- **One follow-up**, five working days later, then leave them alone.

## How to find the next fifty

- **arp-relocation.com/members** lists UK relocation firms, and each
  member page carries a name, a phone number and usually an email. This
  is where four of the ten above came from.
- **Google Maps**, "buying agent" or "relocation consultant" plus a city,
  then the firm's own contact page. Small firms publish an address;
  national chains publish a form.
- **The Property Ombudsman and Propertymark registers** list buying
  agents by name and town.
- For solicitors, **the Law Society's Find a Solicitor** gives firms by
  area of practice, but expect forms rather than addresses.
- Record each one the way the table above does, with the page the
  address came from and the date, so a bounced address can be checked
  rather than guessed at again.

## What counts as a yes

A reply asking for a report is a yes worth chasing. Ten well-aimed emails
that produce one conversation is the normal rate, and one relocation firm
sending three addresses a week is worth more than a hundred consumer
sign-ups. If ten produce nothing, the message is wrong, not the market:
change the first line, not the product.
